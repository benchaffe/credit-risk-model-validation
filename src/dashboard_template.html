Does a mortgage default model survive a crisis?

An independent validation of two probability-of-default models trained on 1999–2004 Freddie Mac loans, then tested on 2005, the 2006–08 crisis and 2016–19. Explore the results below.

Verdict: approved with conditions, for ranking only.
Both models rank borrowers well but under-predict defaults badly when conditions change. Not fit for absolute PD, provisioning or capital use.

Where the models fail worst in the crisis (2006–08)

Observed ÷ predicted default rate for segments of at least 1,000 loans. 1× is perfect. Broker and correspondent loans were almost absent from the development data, so part of that gap is a segment the model never saw.

Ranking holds up better

AUC (higher is better, 0.5 is a coin toss) with 95% intervals. Ranking loses about 0.05–0.07 out of time.

Stress tests

Change in average predicted default rate when one input is shocked (hold-out loans). “Wrong way” is the share of loans where PD moved the opposite way.

Population drift (PSI against development)

Above 0.10 is a moderate shift, above 0.25 significant. Interest rates and loan channel changed completely by 2016–19. Note the score itself barely moved in the crisis, yet it was 4× too low, so PSI alone would have missed it.

Limitations to keep in mind
  • One person developed and validated these models, so this is a self-review, not an independent one.
  • Based on Freddie Mac sample files (50,000 loans per year), not the full dataset.
  • The target excludes COVID-19 forbearance, a modelling choice that affects the 2016–19 results.
  • The data has no protected characteristics, so fairness could only be checked on proxies.
